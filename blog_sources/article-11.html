October 2026 · Eric Kinney

Last week, we sent our CTO to Mexico City for a week of market research.

Andreas Oesterer does not speak Spanish.

He came back with a sombrero, strong opinions about tacos al pastor, and more product clarity than six months of desk research could have produced.

Why We Sent a Human Instead of Reading a Report

There is a version of market validation that involves downloading industry reports, building TAM slides, and citing Statista. We've done that work. We know the numbers.

What you can't get from a report is what it actually feels like to move money in Mexico City in October 2026. Which payment methods work at which merchants. Where the friction is. What the advertising landscape tells you about who is fighting for wallet share — and who isn't. Whether the infrastructure that looks functional in a spreadsheet is functional in practice.

Andreas spent a week finding out.

Andreas, on the right.

What He Found on the Ground

Mexico City doesn't need cash anymore.

This is the first and most important finding. Andreas moved through a full week in one of the largest cities in the world — restaurants, markets, transport, hotels, everything — without using cash once. Mexico City runs on cards and apps. The consumer payment infrastructure that didn't exist a decade ago is now genuinely embedded in daily life.

This matters because the conventional concern about emerging market payment infrastructure is adoption. If consumers aren't already using digital payments, getting them to move money through a new platform is an education problem before it's a product problem. In Mexico City in 2026, that education problem has already been solved by others. The rails exist. The behavior is there.

Consumer fintech advertising is everywhere — and it's loud.

The billboards, the metro ads, the in-app promotions: consumer-facing financial products are being marketed aggressively throughout the city. Gimmicky cashback offers. Flashy card promotions. Rewards programs designed to look like the U.S. consumer fintech playbook, translated.

And a notable observation: a significant presence from Chinese multinational financial brands. Not fringe players. Established companies making serious bets on the Mexican consumer market.

Business payment infrastructure is nearly invisible.

Here is the gap. All of that marketing energy, all of that advertising spend — it's aimed at consumers. The business finance infrastructure that a growing company in Mexico actually needs — reliable cross-border payments, stablecoin rails, multi-currency accounts, working capital tools built for operators rather than consumers — is almost entirely absent from the competitive noise.

The consumer fintech market in Mexico is crowded and loud. The business payment market is quiet in a way that means opportunity, not saturation.

Not every transaction went through.

This is the honest finding that matters most for product development. The rails exist. They are not uniformly reliable. Transaction failures at the point of sale, delays in settlement, inconsistency across different payment methods and merchant categories — these are not theoretical friction points. They are real, and Andreas experienced them directly.

The reliability gap is not a reason to stay out of this market. It is the specific product problem Scurry is built to solve. Fast, reliable, compliant payment infrastructure for businesses operating across the U.S.-Mexico corridor. The market isn't waiting for the rails to exist — it's waiting for the rails to work consistently.

The Numbers Behind What Andreas Saw

The ground-level observations connect directly to data that explains why we're building here.

Mexico is the United States' largest trading partner, with over $800 billion in annual bilateral trade. The U.S.-Mexico remittance corridor processes approximately $65 billion annually — the largest remittance corridor in the world — and the average cost of sending $200 from the U.S. to Mexico is still around 5.4%, according to World Bank data. For a family receiving $500 a month, that's $27 that doesn't arrive. Multiplied across millions of families, across every month, that's a transfer tax on people who can least afford it.

Stablecoin rails can move that money instantly, at a fraction of the cost, with full compliance infrastructure on both ends. That's not a theoretical claim. That's the product.

Mexico's stablecoin adoption is accelerating. In 2024, Mexico ranked among the top ten countries globally for crypto adoption, driven primarily by remittance use cases and inflation hedging. The regulatory environment is evolving — Mexico's AML reform framework, updated earlier this year, is moving toward standards that reward compliant operators and create meaningful barriers for non-compliant ones.

That's the environment Andreas was observing firsthand.

What This Trip Sharpened for Us

We came out of the Mexico City trip with three specific product refinements:

Transaction reliability is a primary feature, not a secondary one. When Andreas experienced failed transactions firsthand, it wasn't a data point — it was a product requirement. Scurry's infrastructure needs to handle the inconsistency of existing rails gracefully, with real-time fallback logic and transparent status communication that keeps the user informed rather than confused. We knew this. Now we felt it.

The business finance gap is our specific lane. The consumer fintech noise validates that digital payment behavior is there. The absence of business-focused competition in the advertising landscape validates that the lane we're in is real and uncrowded. We're not trying to out-cashback a Chinese multinational. We're building the payment infrastructure those same businesses use when they pay suppliers, manage cross-border payroll, or need to move working capital across the border.

Compliance is a competitive advantage in this market, not just a requirement. Mexico's updated AML framework, combined with U.S. FinCEN oversight of the cross-border corridor, means that operating compliantly on both sides of the border is genuinely difficult for new entrants. For Scurry — FinCEN-registered, with an OCC charter in process, built by a founding team with thirty years of BSA experience — that difficulty is our moat. The operators who want a compliant platform to move money across the U.S.-Mexico border don't have many options. We are building one of them.
