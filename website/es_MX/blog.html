The Real Cost of Sending $500 from LA to Mexico City

Let's do the math that Western Union doesn't want you to think about.

You're in Los Angeles. You need $500 to get to your family in Mexico City — for the rent, the groceries, the thing that can't wait until next week. You pull up the app, enter the amount, and see a fee of $5.

Five dollars. That seems reasonable. You confirm.

What you didn't see was the other charge — the one buried in the exchange rate.

The Hidden Tax

Every money transfer company operates the same way. There are two costs: the fee they show you, and the spread they don't.

The spread is the gap between the real exchange rate — the rate you'd see on Google or XE.com, called the mid-market rate — and the rate the company gives you. Western Union, for example, might offer 17.20 pesos per dollar on a $500 transfer when the mid-market rate is 17.75. That 3.1% gap costs you $15.50 in hidden currency conversion fees — more than the $5 explicit fee shown at checkout.

Add the explicit fee plus the FX markup, and a $500 transfer through a Western Union agent in early 2026 typically costs $8-16 in stated fees, plus 1-3% in exchange rate spread on top. All-in: you're looking at $20-35 disappearing from every $500 you send.

MoneyGram is somewhat better on popular corridors like Mexico. A $500 transfer funded by debit card and paid out as cash pickup runs roughly $5 in headline fee plus about 1.6% in exchange rate spread — all-in cost landing near $18. Still $18 gone on a $500 transfer. Still 3.6%.

Now multiply that by twelve months of sending money home. You've paid $216 in fees and spread on $6,000 sent. That's a month of groceries for a family in Mexico City — gone to intermediaries.

Now multiply that by the 40+ million people in the United States with ties to Mexico, many of whom send money home every single month.

Mexico received a record $64.7 billion in remittances in 2024 — the vast majority sent from the United States, making it the single largest remittance corridor in the world. At an average cost of 6.49% of the amount sent, the math is brutal: over $4 billion extracted annually from the people sending it, before it ever reaches the people who need it.

That's not a fee. That's a tax on immigration.

What the Same Transfer Looks Like on Stablecoin Rails

The USDC transfer from the United States to Mexico City doesn't go through a correspondent bank. It doesn't wait for a cut-off window. It doesn't convert through three intermediaries, each taking a slice.

It goes like this:

You send USDC from your Scurry account. On Polygon Chain, the transfer settles in about 5 seconds for an average network fee near $0.002. The USDC arrives in Mexico and converts to pesos through SPEI — Mexico's interbank transfer system, which has access to every bank in the country. The pesos are deposited into the recipient's local bank account completing the transaction swiftly and efficiently — even during nights and weekends, without the need to pre-fund large sums of pesos.

Total elapsed time: minutes, not days.

Total network fee on the transfer itself: less than a penny.

Total cost including on/off-ramp: under 1%.

Stablecoin remittance flows from the US to Mexico quote fees under 1% through services using USDC on Stellar and similar stablecoin rails. Stablecoin-based transfers can reduce remittance costs by as much as 92% compared to traditional channels.

On a $500 transfer: you save $15-30 compared to Western Union. Every time. Every month.

This Isn't Theoretical

This is happening right now, at scale, on the exact corridor we're talking about.

Bitso processed $6.5 billion in U.S.-Mexico crypto remittances in 2024 — roughly 10% of the entire corridor — with competitive FX and same-day settlement. Felix Pago has processed over $1 billion through a USDC-to-SPEI model that settles via WhatsApp, at fees well below Western Union's.

When money is sent through Félix, U.S. dollars paid via debit or ACH are processed through a payment processor, exchanged for USDC, sent to Bitso in Mexico via the low-cost Stellar blockchain, converted into Mexican pesos, and deposited into a local bank account through SPEI — all in just a few seconds.

These aren't pilots. They're production volume. The infrastructure is live. The rails are proven. The only thing that's been missing is a platform that pairs this technology with institutional-grade compliance — one that banking partners, regulators, and the communities being served can all trust.

That's what we're building.

The cost story above is written for individuals. The business story is even more compelling.

"Move Fast and Break Things" Is Over. Here's What We Do Instead.

Silicon Valley has a mantra problem.

"Move fast and break things" was coined by a 19-year-old in a dorm room. It became the defining philosophy of a generation of startups — a permission slip to skip the hard parts, externalize the damage, and call the wreckage disruption.

Mark Zuckerberg eventually abandoned it himself. In 2014, Facebook quietly updated the phrase to "Move fast with stable infrastructure" — which is, if you read it carefully, the opposite of the original.

The industry hasn't caught up.

In fintech specifically, "move fast and break things" has a body count. It's the operating philosophy behind $200 billion in PPP fraud processed by platforms that prioritized loan volume over eligibility controls. It's the mindset that produced Revolut's two-year ECB product restriction — a company with a $200 billion valuation and every resource available, still unable to build a compliance culture because the CEO described his staff as "self-guided missiles." It's the attitude that turns a BSA Officer into an obstacle and a SAR into a problem.

I've spent thirty years watching what breaks when that's the mantra. Now I'm building something with a different one.

What the Navy SEALs Understood

I come from a world where speed is sacred — but where the definition of speed is more precise than Silicon Valley's.

The Navy SEALs have a phrase that sounds like a paradox: "Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast."

It means this: if you move in haste, you create errors. Errors require correction. Correction costs more time than the haste saved. So the team that moves deliberately — that plans meticulously, executes cleanly, and absorbs friction before it becomes failure — arrives faster than the team that sprinted and stumbled.

In a SEAL operation, one person's haste can cause the next person to trip, and the next after that. The whole unit slows to recover what one person's urgency broke.

In a fintech, one executive's growth pressure can cause a BSA Officer to get overruled, a SAR to go unfiled, a fraud pattern to go undetected. And the whole institution slows — by years, by consent orders, by enforcement actions — to recover what one quarter's urgency broke.

The SEALs figured this out in high-stakes physical environments. It applies with equal force to high-stakes financial ones.

The Mantra Isn't Enough on Its Own

"Slow is smooth, smooth is fast" is operationally correct. But for a company building in emerging markets — where 1.7 billion people are waiting for access to stable financial infrastructure, where the U.S.-Mexico remittance corridor processes hundreds of billions of dollars a year at punishing cost, where regulatory clarity is finally arriving after years of ambiguity — deliberateness alone isn't the complete philosophy.

We also need curiosity. We need to keep asking why the system works the way it works, and whether it has to. We need to keep finding the family in Los Angeles paying $30 to send $500 home and asking whether that's inevitable — or just unexamined.

We need discipline in our experiments. Not "move fast and break things" — but test deliberately, instrument everything, and apply what you learn to the next decision. The difference between experimentation and recklessness is whether you're paying attention to what breaks and why.

And we need resilience. Emerging markets are not easy markets. Regulatory frameworks are still developing. Banking partnerships require relationship-building that doesn't happen in a sprint. The communities we're serving have been failed by institutions that couldn't sustain the commitment. Resilience isn't a virtue in this context — it's a prerequisite.

What We Built Instead

At Scurry, we have a mantra.

It came out of the same place as everything else we've built — the thirty years of watching compliance fail, the SEAL training that taught operational patience, the open water swims that require you to move through resistance rather than against it, and the communities in Mexico and LATAM who have waited long enough for someone to do this right.

Build right. Move far.

Two words each side. A tension that resolves.

Build right: compliance infrastructure before user acquisition. AML program before product launch. KYC and sanctions screening as core architecture, not afterthought. The kind of institutional foundation that lets you walk into a banking partner meeting and answer every question they have before they ask it.

Move far: not move fast. Far. Across the U.S.-Mexico corridor. Into Argentina, Colombia, the Philippines. To the 1.7 billion people the current financial system has decided aren't worth serving. To the markets that the "move fast" companies never reached because they were too busy breaking things closer to home.

The companies that moved fast broke things. The companies that built right moved far.

That's the only thesis worth building on.

How This Shows Up in the Scurry World

Some of you know me from the book series — Scurry (Fraud Squad) and the upcoming Scurry: Fraud Syndicate. The protagonist in those novels is a former Navy SEAL turned fintech BSA Officer who runs half-marathons at 5 AM and sees money laundering patterns the way he once spotted Taliban financing networks in Kandahar.

He doesn't move fast. He moves smooth.

He plans. He documents. He files the SAR when every executive in the building is telling him not to. And he arrives — at the truth, at the evidence, at the outcome — faster than the people who skipped those steps.

The fiction and the company are built from the same thesis. You can't move far if you keep breaking things along the way.

A Note to the Founders Reading This

If you're building in fintech right now — stablecoins, neobanks, payments infrastructure, anything that touches money — you have a choice that didn't exist ten years ago.

The regulatory environment is clearer. The GENIUS Act has arrived. Banking partners are actively looking for compliant stablecoin platforms. The market is rewarding institutional trust in ways it didn't during the peak "move fast" era.

You can still choose to move fast and break things. People will fund it. For a while.

Or you can build right and move far.

We chose the second one. We'd like more company.

What Is a Stablecoin? (And Why Does It Matter for Your Business in Mexico?)

If you run a business with any connection to Mexico — paying suppliers, receiving payments from U.S. customers, managing payroll across the border, or simply trying to protect your cash from currency volatility — you've probably felt the friction.

The wire that takes three days. The 5-6% that disappears into fees and exchange rate spreads before your money arrives. The cut-off window you missed because your bank closes at 3pm. The supplier in Monterrey who still wants to be paid in pesos, which means you're converting dollars to USDC to pesos and losing something at every step.

That friction isn't a technology problem. It's an infrastructure problem. And stablecoins are the infrastructure solution that's already working — right now, at scale, on the U.S.-Mexico corridor.

Here's what you need to know.

What Is a Stablecoin?

A stablecoin is a digital currency pegged 1:1 to a stable asset — usually the U.S. dollar. One USDC equals one dollar. Always. It doesn't fluctuate the way Bitcoin does. It doesn't lose value overnight. It's the dollar, running on blockchain rails instead of correspondent bank rails.

Think of it this way: the U.S. dollar is the most trusted currency in the world. A dollar stablecoin is that same dollar, but able to move anywhere on earth in minutes, at a fraction of the cost, 24 hours a day, seven days a week, without waiting for a bank to open or a wire to clear.

That's not a cryptocurrency speculation play. That's a payment rail.

The two dominant stablecoins in business use today are USDC (issued by Circle, backed 1:1 by cash and U.S. Treasuries with monthly attestations) and USDT (Tether, the most traded stablecoin globally). For businesses operating in regulated environments — and for any platform with serious Anti Money Laundering and compliance requirements — USDC is the institutional-grade choice. It's the one Visa is now settling on. It's the one Nubank embedded as a core product for its 127 million customers across LATAM. It's the one we built Scurry around.

Why This Matters Specifically for Mexico

Mexico's remittance corridor with the United States is the fourth largest in the world — $42.8 billion flowing annually, most of it from Mexican families and workers in California and Texas sending money home.

The average cost of sending that money through traditional channels: 6.49% of the amount sent. On a $500 transfer, that's $32 gone before it arrives. Multiply that across millions of transfers and you're looking at billions of dollars extracted annually from people who can least afford it.

Stablecoins compress that cost by 80-90%.

Bitso, Mexico's largest crypto exchange, processed $6.5 billion in U.S.-Mexico crypto remittances in 2024 — roughly 10% of the entire corridor — with competitive FX and same-day settlement. Felix Pago has processed over $1 billion through a USDC-to-SPEI model that settles via WhatsApp, at fees well below Western Union's. BBVA Mexico reported 450% growth in USDC transaction volume. Stablecoins now account for approximately 8% of Mexico's entire remittance market — and the infrastructure is accelerating.

This isn't a pilot. It's not a proof of concept. It's live volume, moving through regulated rails, settling in minutes.

What This Means for Your Business

The remittance story gets the headlines. But the business case for stablecoins in Mexico goes far beyond families sending money home.

If you're a U.S. business paying Mexican suppliers: A traditional wire to a supplier in CDMX or Monterrey typically takes 1-3 business days, costs 2-5% in fees and FX markups, requires a cut-off submission before 3pm on a business day, and generates reconciliation paperwork that your treasury team spends hours on. A USDC settlement reaches the same supplier in minutes, at a fraction of the cost, any time of day, any day of the week — with a clean on-chain record for reconciliation.

If you're a Mexican business receiving payments from U.S. clients: You've been at the mercy of correspondent banking delays, FX conversion spreads, and a settlement calendar that doesn't respect your cash flow needs. Stablecoin rails flip that dynamic. You receive in USDC, convert to pesos through local rails (SPEI settlement is already live), and the entire cycle takes hours, not days.

If you're managing cross-border payroll: Stablecoins settle 24/7. Your contractor in Guadalajara doesn't wait until Monday for a payment you sent on Friday. Your treasury doesn't pre-fund accounts across multiple currencies. You position liquidity on-chain and move it when you need it.

If you're worried about FX volatility: The peso's exposure to U.S. monetary policy and local inflation is structural. A USDC-denominated treasury position gives your business dollar stability without requiring a U.S. bank account or a complex hedging strategy.

The Regulatory Picture — Right Now

One of the consistent objections to stablecoins in business contexts is regulatory uncertainty. That objection is increasingly outdated.

In the United States, the GENIUS Act — signed July 18, 2025 — established the first federal framework for payment stablecoins: 1:1 reserves, monthly attestations, and AML compliance requirements. The jurisdictional ambiguity that slowed institutional adoption in 2023 and 2024 is largely resolved.

In Mexico, the Ley Fintech (fully implemented in 2024) authorized 23 institutions for stablecoin services. B2B stablecoin operators function under Mexico's AML regime as non-financial entities — workable today, with regulatory modernization actively underway. The corridor between the U.S. and Mexico is one of the most active stablecoin corridors in the world precisely because the regulatory foundations on both ends are increasingly clear.

This is the window. The rails are built. The regulatory frameworks are arriving. The businesses that move now are the ones that capture the cost and speed advantage before this becomes table stakes.

We designed our KYC framework, sanctions screening, and transaction monitoring as core infrastructure — not an afterthought added when a banking partner asked for it.
