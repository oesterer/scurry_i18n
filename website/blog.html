The GENIUS Act: What It Actually Means for Stablecoin Businesses

On July 18, 2025, President Trump signed the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act — the GENIUS Act — into law. It passed with unusual bipartisan strength: 68-30 in the Senate, 308-122 in the House.

That vote count matters. This wasn't a partisan crypto bill. It was a broad congressional consensus that the regulatory ambiguity surrounding stablecoins had lasted long enough, and that the United States needed a clear framework before the rest of the world built one without us.

For businesses considering stablecoin rails — and for anyone building stablecoin infrastructure — the GENIUS Act is the most important regulatory development in the history of digital payments. Here's what it actually says, what it still leaves open, and what it means for how Scurry is built.

What the GENIUS Act Actually Does

Before the Act, stablecoin issuers operated in a regulatory gray area. Depending on who you asked, they were money transmitters, unregistered securities issuers, bank-like entities, or none of the above. That ambiguity made banking partners nervous, institutional adoption slow, and compliance programs inconsistent.

The GENIUS Act resolves the fundamental question: what is a payment stablecoin, and who can issue one?

Definition: A payment stablecoin is a digital asset issued for payment or settlement, redeemable at a fixed predetermined value (one dollar equals one dollar), backed 1:1 by high-quality reserves. Crucially, the Act states that payment stablecoins issued by permitted issuers are not securities or commodities— ending years of jurisdictional uncertainty between the SEC and CFTC.

Who can issue: Only “Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuers” — entities that have obtained either a federal license or a qualifying state license. State-qualified issuers with outstanding issuance of not more than $10 billion may operate under a state-level regulatory regime, but only where that regime is “substantially similar” to the federal framework. Exceed $10 billion and you transition to federal oversight.

The Five Requirements That Matter Most

1. 1:1 Reserve Backing — No Exceptions

Issuers must hold at least one dollar of permitted reserves for every one dollar of stablecoins issued. Those reserves are limited to specific high-quality, liquid assets: U.S. coins and currency, insured bank deposits, short-dated Treasury bills, repurchase agreements backed by Treasuries, government money market funds, central bank reserves, and similar government-issued assets approved by regulators.

No fractional reserve. No holding crypto as a reserve asset. No creative accounting. One dollar in, one dollar held. This is what makes a payment stablecoin trustworthy at scale — and it's what distinguishes regulated stablecoins from the algorithmic experiments that collapsed the crypto market in 2022.

2. Monthly Reserve Reporting with Executive Sign-Off

Issuers must publish monthly reports detailing reserve composition and total stablecoins outstanding, with CEO and CFO certifications of accuracy. These reports must be examined monthly by independent registered public accounting firms.

This is a higher reporting standard than most public companies face. Monthly attestations, independently examined, signed by the CEO and CFO. If your reserves don't match your outstanding stablecoins, it's not a disclosure issue — it's a federal compliance violation.

3. Full BSA/AML Treatment

For purposes of the Bank Secrecy Act, a payment stablecoin issuer will be considered a financial institution and must implement a robust AML and sanctions compliance program, including the designation of an officer to supervise the program.

This is the provision that matters most to us at Scurry — and the one that most directly validates the compliance-first approach we've taken from day one. Stablecoin issuers are now explicitly financial institutions under the BSA. That means customer identification programs, transaction monitoring, suspicious activity reporting, and a designated compliance officer. Not optional. Not scalable later. Required from the moment you issue.

Issuers must submit a certification of AML program effectiveness to their regulator within 180 days of licensure and annually thereafter — a formal attestation requirement that goes beyond simply having policies on paper.

4. Sanctions Compliance — Now Mandatory by Law

Issuers must maintain effective economic sanctions compliance programs, including the verification of sanctions lists— and must maintain the technical capability to comply with lawful orders to block, seize, freeze, burn, or prevent the transfer of outstanding stablecoins.

This is the first time that sanctions compliance programs have been mandated by law for stablecoin issuers. The ability to freeze or burn tokens on-chain isn't just a product feature — it's now a regulatory requirement.

5. Prohibition on Convicted Financial Criminals

The Act prohibits anyone convicted of certain financial crimes from serving as an officer or director of a stablecoin issuer. This is the provision that should have existed in fintech generally and for years didn't. It's a governance floor, not a ceiling.

What the GENIUS Act Still Leaves Open

The Act is historic, but it's not complete. Several critical questions remain unresolved — and these are the questions that compliance teams building on stablecoin rails need to track carefully.

Unhosted wallets and peer-to-peer transfers: Nothing in the GENIUS Act addresses the treatment of unhosted wallets and peer-to-peer transfers for payment purposes, or the duty of virtual asset service providers to comply with the travel rule in respect of transfers to or from unhosted wallets. This is a significant gap. The travel rule — which requires financial institutions to pass identifying information along with transactions above certain thresholds — is a core BSA obligation, and its application to wallet-to-wallet transfers on public blockchains is still unresolved.

FinCEN's tailored AML rules: The Act requires FinCEN to write rules tailored to the size and complexity of stablecoin issuers — but those rules are still being developed. On April 3, 2026, the Treasury Department issued a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking setting forth broad-based principles for determining whether a state-level regulatory regime is substantially similar to the federal framework. We're still in the comment and rulemaking period. The final rules will matter enormously for how compliance programs are structured in practice.

Foreign issuers: The Act includes AML and sanctions compliance provisions that require foreign issuers to meet AML and sanctions compliance requirements before issuing payment stablecoins to holders in the United States. The extraterritorial reach of the GENIUS Act — and what “substantially similar” compliance looks like for a non-U.S. issuer — is an open question with significant implications for cross-border stablecoin platforms operating in corridors like U.S.-Mexico, where multiple regulatory regimes intersect.

What This Means for Businesses Using Stablecoin Rails

If you're a business evaluating stablecoin payment infrastructure — whether for cross-border supplier payments, payroll, or treasury management — the GENIUS Act changes the risk calculus significantly.

The upside: The regulatory clarity you've been waiting for has arrived. Payment stablecoins are not securities. The reserve requirements, reporting standards, and compliance obligations are defined. Banking partners who were hesitant to work with stablecoin platforms now have a federal framework to point to when their own regulators ask questions. The institutional adoption that's been building — Visa settling on USDC, Nubank embedding stablecoins for 127 million customers — accelerates under clear rules.

The due diligence question: Not all stablecoin platforms are GENIUS Act compliant. The Act creates a licensed issuer framework — which means the question to ask any stablecoin provider is simple: are you a Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuer, or are you building toward that status? What does your reserve backing look like? Who is your designated compliance officer? When was your AML program last certified?

If a stablecoin platform can't answer those questions clearly, the GENIUS Act has effectively made that platform a compliance liability for the businesses using it.

How Scurry Is Built for This Environment

We registered Scurry as a Money Services Business with FinCEN before we acquired our first user. Our AML program, KYC framework, and sanctions screening were operational before our product launched. We designated a compliance officer — that's Eric Kinney, thirty years as a BSA Officer and Certified Fraud Examiner, DOJ witness in a major fintech fraud case — before we built anything else.

The GENIUS Act didn't change how we build. It validated why we built this way from the start.

The compliance requirements the Act mandates — 1:1 reserves, monthly reporting, full BSA treatment, sanctions program, designated compliance officer — are not new burdens for Scurry. They are descriptions of infrastructure we already have.

For the businesses and banking partners evaluating stablecoin platforms in this new regulatory environment: that's the difference that matters. Not who moves fastest. Who built it right.

The window to build compliant stablecoin infrastructure for emerging markets is open. The regulatory framework has arrived. The question now is which platforms can operate within it — and which ones are still catching up.

Build right. Move far.

Argentina, Inflation, and Why Stablecoins Aren't Crypto to The Hustlers

Argentina just made the World Cup final again.

In Buenos Aires, the celebration was the kind that spills into the streets for days — asado, flags, mate, the particular Argentine conviction that this time, this team, this moment is different.

And then Monday came. The peso was still the peso.

That contrast — the global ambition and the monetary reality — is the thing I kept coming back to during the weeks I spent in Argentina as part of my year studying and travelling across Latin America. People there are not naive about their currency. They haven't been for decades. What struck me wasn't despair about it. It was the sophistication of the workarounds. The matter-of-fact fluency with which ordinary people — engineers, teachers, small business owners — had built their own parallel financial infrastructure to protect themselves from a currency that the government couldn't stabilize.

“Tenemos que sobrevivir,” one founder told me over Mate in Palermo. We have to survive.

USDC was his savings account.

The Numbers Behind the Survival Strategy

Argentina has experienced inflation exceeding 200% annually in recent years. With inflation topping 140% before the 2023 presidential election, Argentine citizens were already turning to more radical measures — and the conditions have continued to demand creative responses since.

An estimated 19.8% of Argentines now own cryptocurrency— one of the highest per-capita rates in the world. Argentina leads the world in stablecoin adoption per capita, driven by peso devaluation and a generation of dollar-denominated savings habits.

This is not speculation. This is not people betting on token prices. In urban centers like Buenos Aires, stablecoins are increasingly used for receiving salaries and small transactions — a survival strategy circumventing the high fees and political risk associated with traditional financial institutions.

Argentines hold USDT and USDC as the household dollar because the alternative — a peso held in a bank savings account — is a guaranteed real loss. The Milei administration's capital control relaxations in 2025 changed some of the mechanics, but the underlying dynamic remains: the peso is a depreciating asset, and USDC is the stable alternative that anyone with a smartphone can access.

Argentina's P2P USDT market has effectively replaced the informal blue dollar exchange that previously required physical cash and personal trust networks. What used to require a cueva — an informal money exchange operating in the gray market — now runs on an app. The trust network still matters. The friction has dropped dramatically.

What We Heard on the Ground

Before we finalized Scurry's product direction, we spent time talking to Argentines — not just in Buenos Aires, but in the Bay Area, where a significant Argentine diaspora of engineers, founders, restaurant owners and tech workers gave us a window into how the problem actually feels from the inside.

The conversations were consistent in ways that no market research report could have predicted.

Nobody described USDC as “crypto.” That word carries connotations of speculation, volatility, and the FTX collapse — none of which map to how a software engineer in Menlo Park thinks about the USDC balance she uses to protect her Buenos Aires savings from devaluation. She described it the way someone in the United States might describe a savings account. Stable. Accessible. Hers.

What they described needing wasn't a trading platform. It was a bank account that held dollars — reliably, accessibly, without requiring a U.S. Social Security number or a U.S. address or a relationship with a U.S. bank that they couldn't get. A financial home base in the currency they trusted, operated by an institution that understood their situation.

That's the product we're building.

One Argentine founder we spoke with in the Bay Area showed us his Binance P2P setup — the workaround he used to convert pesos to USDC across borders without triggering capital controls. It was functional. It was five steps too many. It required trust in counterparties he'd never met. And it had no consumer protection, no dispute resolution, and no compliance structure that gave him any recourse if something went wrong.

“If someone built this properly,” he said, “with a real compliance program and a real bank behind it, I would use it for everything.”

That conversation is part of why Scurry exists.

The De Facto Dollarization No One Planned

Argentina's government has debated official dollarization for years. President Milei ran on it. While the continued discussion between a CBDC and dollarization demonstrates Argentina's pursuit of monetary stability, the Argentines have already adopted the digital dollar — via USDT and USDC.

The bottom-up version happened without a policy decision. It happened because people needed it to happen. Stablecoins now process over $46 trillion in annual transactions globally, rivaling Visa and SWIFT — and a disproportionate share of that volume represents genuine economic activity in emerging markets, not speculative trading.

The province of Mendoza already accepts tax payments in stablecoins — a signal that even local governments are adapting to the reality their citizens have already created.

This is what “The Hustlers” — one of Scurry's three core customer segments — actually looks like. Not a monolithic category of “emerging market users,” but a software engineer in Palermo protecting six months of savings from a devaluation she knows is coming. A freelancer in Córdoba who invoices her U.S. clients in dollars and needs those dollars to stay dollars until she decides to spend them. A small business owner in Rosario who buys inventory from suppliers in Brazil and needs a stable unit of account that doesn't require converting through two volatile currencies.

These are people who have figured out the solution on their own — imperfectly, with too much friction, through platforms that weren't built for them. What they need is the infrastructure layer that makes the solution elegant, compliant, and permanent.

What the Compliance Layer Makes Possible

Here's the thing that the informal workarounds can't provide: recourse.

If your Binance P2P counterparty disappears with your pesos, you have no claim. If an informal exchange gives you a bad rate and pockets the difference, you have no audit trail. If your stablecoin platform operates without KYC, without AML controls, without a compliance officer who has to answer to a regulator — you have no protection when something goes wrong.

With inflation often exceeding 200% annually, peso salaries can lose value quickly, while banking delays and conversion friction create additional risk. Stablecoin payroll can remove friction, but only if you preserve compliant reporting and valuation discipline.

The compliance infrastructure isn't the obstacle to financial inclusion in Argentina. It's the prerequisite for the kind of institutional trust that makes financial inclusion sustainable. The informal dollar economy has existed in Argentina for decades precisely because the formal dollar economy was inaccessible. Stablecoins can bridge that gap — but only if the platform operating them is built to the standard that regulators, banking partners, and the users themselves can rely on.

That's what Scurry brings to this market. FinCEN-registered MSB. AML program operational before user acquisition. KYC framework designed for the emerging market user who may have a national ID and a smartphone but not a U.S. bank account. The compliance infrastructure that makes stablecoin access not just possible but trustworthy.

Why Argentina Is in Scurry's Roadmap

We're starting with the U.S.-Mexico corridor because the infrastructure is most developed and the volume is largest. But Argentina is not far behind — and in some ways, the case for stablecoins is even more urgent there.

Mexico's remittance problem is about cost and speed. Argentina's stablecoin problem is about survival. Those are different emotional registers, and they demand slightly different product responses — but the underlying infrastructure is the same. Dollar-stable, compliant, accessible from a smartphone, with recourse when something goes wrong.

El dólar siempre gana — the dollar always wins — is something Argentines have understood for longer than most. The question has always been how to access it.

The Real Cost of Sending $500 from LA to Mexico City

Let's do the math that Western Union doesn't want you to think about.

You're in Los Angeles. You need $500 to get to your family in Mexico City — for the rent, the groceries, the thing that can't wait until next week. You pull up the app, enter the amount, and see a fee of $5.

Five dollars. That seems reasonable. You confirm.

What you didn't see was the other charge — the one buried in the exchange rate.

The Hidden Tax

Every money transfer company operates the same way. There are two costs: the fee they show you, and the spread they don't.

The spread is the gap between the real exchange rate — the rate you'd see on Google or XE.com, called the mid-market rate — and the rate the company gives you. Western Union, for example, might offer 17.20 pesos per dollar on a $500 transfer when the mid-market rate is 17.75. That 3.1% gap costs you $15.50 in hidden currency conversion fees — more than the $5 explicit fee shown at checkout.

Add the explicit fee plus the FX markup, and a $500 transfer through a Western Union agent in early 2026 typically costs $8-16 in stated fees, plus 1-3% in exchange rate spread on top. All-in: you're looking at $20-35 disappearing from every $500 you send.

MoneyGram is somewhat better on popular corridors like Mexico. A $500 transfer funded by debit card and paid out as cash pickup runs roughly $5 in headline fee plus about 1.6% in exchange rate spread — all-in cost landing near $18. Still $18 gone on a $500 transfer. Still 3.6%.

Now multiply that by twelve months of sending money home. You've paid $216 in fees and spread on $6,000 sent. That's a month of groceries for a family in Mexico City — gone to intermediaries.

Now multiply that by the 40+ million people in the United States with ties to Mexico, many of whom send money home every single month.

Mexico received a record $64.7 billion in remittances in 2024 — the vast majority sent from the United States, making it the single largest remittance corridor in the world. At an average cost of 6.49% of the amount sent, the math is brutal: over $4 billion extracted annually from the people sending it, before it ever reaches the people who need it.

That's not a fee. That's a tax on immigration.

What the Same Transfer Looks Like on Stablecoin Rails

The USDC transfer from the United States to Mexico City doesn't go through a correspondent bank. It doesn't wait for a cut-off window. It doesn't convert through three intermediaries, each taking a slice.

It goes like this:

You send USDC from your Scurry account. On Polygon Chain, the transfer settles in about 5 seconds for an average network fee near $0.002. The USDC arrives in Mexico and converts to pesos through SPEI — Mexico's interbank transfer system, which has access to every bank in the country. The pesos are deposited into the recipient's local bank account completing the transaction swiftly and efficiently — even during nights and weekends, without the need to pre-fund large sums of pesos.

Total elapsed time: minutes, not days.

Total network fee on the transfer itself: less than a penny.

Total cost including on/off-ramp: under 1%.

Stablecoin remittance flows from the US to Mexico quote fees under 1% through services using USDC on Stellar and similar stablecoin rails. Stablecoin-based transfers can reduce remittance costs by as much as 92% compared to traditional channels.

On a $500 transfer: you save $15-30 compared to Western Union. Every time. Every month.

This Isn't Theoretical

This is happening right now, at scale, on the exact corridor we're talking about.

Bitso processed $6.5 billion in U.S.-Mexico crypto remittances in 2024 — roughly 10% of the entire corridor — with competitive FX and same-day settlement. Felix Pago has processed over $1 billion through a USDC-to-SPEI model that settles via WhatsApp, at fees well below Western Union's.

When money is sent through Félix, U.S. dollars paid via debit or ACH are processed through a payment processor, exchanged for USDC, sent to Bitso in Mexico via the low-cost Stellar blockchain, converted into Mexican pesos, and deposited into a local bank account through SPEI — all in just a few seconds.

These aren't pilots. They're production volume. The infrastructure is live. The rails are proven. The only thing that's been missing is a platform that pairs this technology with institutional-grade compliance — one that banking partners, regulators, and the communities being served can all trust.

That's what we're building.

What This Means for Your Business

The cost story above is written for individuals. The business story is even more compelling.

"Move Fast and Break Things" Is Over. Here's What We Do Instead.

Silicon Valley has a mantra problem.

"Move fast and break things" was coined by a 19-year-old in a dorm room. It became the defining philosophy of a generation of startups — a permission slip to skip the hard parts, externalize the damage, and call the wreckage disruption.

Mark Zuckerberg eventually abandoned it himself. In 2014, Facebook quietly updated the phrase to "Move fast with stable infrastructure" — which is, if you read it carefully, the opposite of the original.

The industry hasn't caught up.

In fintech specifically, "move fast and break things" has a body count. It's the operating philosophy behind $200 billion in PPP fraud processed by platforms that prioritized loan volume over eligibility controls. It's the mindset that produced Revolut's two-year ECB product restriction — a company with a $200 billion valuation and every resource available, still unable to build a compliance culture because the CEO described his staff as "self-guided missiles." It's the attitude that turns a BSA Officer into an obstacle and a SAR into a problem.

I've spent thirty years watching what breaks when that's the mantra. Now I'm building something with a different one.

What the Navy SEALs Understood

I come from a world where speed is sacred — but where the definition of speed is more precise than Silicon Valley's.

The Navy SEALs have a phrase that sounds like a paradox: "Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast."

It means this: if you move in haste, you create errors. Errors require correction. Correction costs more time than the haste saved. So the team that moves deliberately — that plans meticulously, executes cleanly, and absorbs friction before it becomes failure — arrives faster than the team that sprinted and stumbled.

In a SEAL operation, one person's haste can cause the next person to trip, and the next after that. The whole unit slows to recover what one person's urgency broke.

In a fintech, one executive's growth pressure can cause a BSA Officer to get overruled, a SAR to go unfiled, a fraud pattern to go undetected. And the whole institution slows — by years, by consent orders, by enforcement actions — to recover what one quarter's urgency broke.

The SEALs figured this out in high-stakes physical environments. It applies with equal force to high-stakes financial ones.

The Mantra Isn't Enough on Its Own

"Slow is smooth, smooth is fast" is operationally correct. But for a company building in emerging markets — where 1.7 billion people are waiting for access to stable financial infrastructure, where the U.S.-Mexico remittance corridor processes hundreds of billions of dollars a year at punishing cost, where regulatory clarity is finally arriving after years of ambiguity — deliberateness alone isn't the complete philosophy.

We also need curiosity. We need to keep asking why the system works the way it works, and whether it has to. We need to keep finding the family in Los Angeles paying $30 to send $500 home and asking whether that's inevitable — or just unexamined.

We need discipline in our experiments. Not "move fast and break things" — but test deliberately, instrument everything, and apply what you learn to the next decision. The difference between experimentation and recklessness is whether you're paying attention to what breaks and why.

And we need resilience. Emerging markets are not easy markets. Regulatory frameworks are still developing. Banking partnerships require relationship-building that doesn't happen in a sprint. The communities we're serving have been failed by institutions that couldn't sustain the commitment. Resilience isn't a virtue in this context — it's a prerequisite.

What We Built Instead

At Scurry, we have a mantra.

It came out of the same place as everything else we've built — the thirty years of watching compliance fail, the SEAL training that taught operational patience, the open water swims that require you to move through resistance rather than against it, and the communities in Mexico and LATAM who have waited long enough for someone to do this right.

Build right. Move far.

Two words each side. A tension that resolves.

Build right: compliance infrastructure before user acquisition. AML program before product launch. KYC and sanctions screening as core architecture, not afterthought. The kind of institutional foundation that lets you walk into a banking partner meeting and answer every question they have before they ask it.

Move far: not move fast. Far. Across the U.S.-Mexico corridor. Into Argentina, Colombia, the Philippines. To the 1.7 billion people the current financial system has decided aren't worth serving. To the markets that the "move fast" companies never reached because they were too busy breaking things closer to home.

The companies that moved fast broke things. The companies that built right moved far.

That's the only thesis worth building on.

How This Shows Up in the Scurry World

Some of you know me from the book series — Scurry (Fraud Squad) and the upcoming Scurry: Fraud Syndicate. The protagonist in those novels is a former Navy SEAL turned fintech BSA Officer who runs half-marathons at 5 AM and sees money laundering patterns the way he once spotted Taliban financing networks in Kandahar.

If you're building in fintech right now — stablecoins, neobanks, payments infrastructure, anything that touches money — you have a choice that didn't exist ten years ago.

The regulatory environment is clearer. The GENIUS Act has arrived. Banking partners are actively looking for compliant stablecoin platforms. The market is rewarding institutional trust in ways it didn't during the peak "move fast" era.

What Is a Stablecoin? (And Why Does It Matter for Your Business in Mexico?)

If you run a business with any connection to Mexico — paying suppliers, receiving payments from U.S. customers, managing payroll across the border, or simply trying to protect your cash from currency volatility — you've probably felt the friction.

The wire that takes three days. The 5-6% that disappears into fees and exchange rate spreads before your money arrives. The cut-off window you missed because your bank closes at 3pm. The supplier in Monterrey who still wants to be paid in pesos, which means you're converting dollars to USDC to pesos and losing something at every step.

That friction isn't a technology problem. It's an infrastructure problem. And stablecoins are the infrastructure solution that's already working — right now, at scale, on the U.S.-Mexico corridor.

Here's what you need to know.

What Is a Stablecoin?

A stablecoin is a digital currency pegged 1:1 to a stable asset — usually the U.S. dollar. One USDC equals one dollar. Always. It doesn't fluctuate the way Bitcoin does. It doesn't lose value overnight. It's the dollar, running on blockchain rails instead of correspondent bank rails.

Think of it this way: the U.S. dollar is the most trusted currency in the world. A dollar stablecoin is that same dollar, but able to move anywhere on earth in minutes, at a fraction of the cost, 24 hours a day, seven days a week, without waiting for a bank to open or a wire to clear.

That's not a cryptocurrency speculation play. That's a payment rail.

The two dominant stablecoins in business use today are USDC (issued by Circle, backed 1:1 by cash and U.S. Treasuries with monthly attestations) and USDT (Tether, the most traded stablecoin globally). For businesses operating in regulated environments — and for any platform with serious Anti Money Laundering and compliance requirements — USDC is the institutional-grade choice. It's the one Visa is now settling on. It's the one Nubank embedded as a core product for its 127 million customers across LATAM. It's the one we built Scurry around.

Why This Matters Specifically for Mexico

Mexico's remittance corridor with the United States is the fourth largest in the world — $42.8 billion flowing annually, most of it from Mexican families and workers in California and Texas sending money home.

The average cost of sending that money through traditional channels: 6.49% of the amount sent. On a $500 transfer, that's $32 gone before it arrives. Multiply that across millions of transfers and you're looking at billions of dollars extracted annually from people who can least afford it.

Stablecoins compress that cost by 80-90%.

Bitso, Mexico's largest crypto exchange, processed $6.5 billion in U.S.-Mexico crypto remittances in 2024 — roughly 10% of the entire corridor — with competitive FX and same-day settlement. Felix Pago has processed over $1 billion through a USDC-to-SPEI model that settles via WhatsApp, at fees well below Western Union's. BBVA Mexico reported 450% growth in USDC transaction volume. Stablecoins now account for approximately 8% of Mexico's entire remittance market — and the infrastructure is accelerating.

This isn't a pilot. It's not a proof of concept. It's live volume, moving through regulated rails, settling in minutes.

What This Means for Your Business

The remittance story gets the headlines. But the business case for stablecoins in Mexico goes far beyond families sending money home.

If you're a U.S. business paying Mexican suppliers: A traditional wire to a supplier in CDMX or Monterrey typically takes 1-3 business days, costs 2-5% in fees and FX markups, requires a cut-off submission before 3pm on a business day, and generates reconciliation paperwork that your treasury team spends hours on. A USDC settlement reaches the same supplier in minutes, at a fraction of the cost, any time of day, any day of the week — with a clean on-chain record for reconciliation.

If you're a Mexican business receiving payments from U.S. clients: You've been at the mercy of correspondent banking delays, FX conversion spreads, and a settlement calendar that doesn't respect your cash flow needs. Stablecoin rails flip that dynamic. You receive in USDC, convert to pesos through local rails (SPEI settlement is already live), and the entire cycle takes hours, not days.

If you're managing cross-border payroll: Stablecoins settle 24/7. Your contractor in Guadalajara doesn't wait until Monday for a payment you sent on Friday. Your treasury doesn't pre-fund accounts across multiple currencies. You position liquidity on-chain and move it when you need it.

If you're worried about FX volatility: The peso's exposure to U.S. monetary policy and local inflation is structural. A USDC-denominated treasury position gives your business dollar stability without requiring a U.S. bank account or a complex hedging strategy.

The Regulatory Picture — Right Now

One of the consistent objections to stablecoins in business contexts is regulatory uncertainty. That objection is increasingly outdated.

In the United States, the GENIUS Act — signed July 18, 2025 — established the first federal framework for payment stablecoins: 1:1 reserves, monthly attestations, and AML compliance requirements. The jurisdictional ambiguity that slowed institutional adoption in 2023 and 2024 is largely resolved.

In Mexico, the Ley Fintech (fully implemented in 2024) authorized 23 institutions for stablecoin services. B2B stablecoin operators function under Mexico's AML regime as non-financial entities — workable today, with regulatory modernization actively underway. The corridor between the U.S. and Mexico is one of the most active stablecoin corridors in the world precisely because the regulatory foundations on both ends are increasingly clear.

This is the window. The rails are built. The regulatory frameworks are arriving. The businesses that move now are the ones that capture the cost and speed advantage before this becomes table stakes.
